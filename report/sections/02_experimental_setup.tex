\section{Experimental setup and datasets}
\subsection{Detector modules and acquisition}
Two opposing detector modules comprise silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) coupled to
lutetium--yttrium oxyorthosilicate (LYSO) crystals of dimensions
$2\times2\times3\,\mathrm{mm}^{3}$. A $^{22}\mathrm{Na}$ source is positioned centrally
between the modules. The University of Chicago (UC) and Fondazione Bruno Kessler (FBK)
measurements use the same crystals and SiPMs; the two arrangements differ in their
analog front-end (AFE) electronics.

Figure~\ref{fig:amplification-stages} shows the readout chain from the LYSO crystal
and SiPM through the two cascaded AFE amplification stages. The first amplifier, or preamplifier,
provides initial amplification and an accessible intermediate waveform. The second
amplifier, or postamplifier, processes this signal with additional gain and provides the
final waveform. Both outputs are acquired simultaneously for each detector. They
originate from the same SiPM pulse and are correlated, while the subsequent amplification
can modify the amplitude, bandwidth, noise and temporal characteristics. The final
waveform can therefore differ in shape as well as amplitude from the intermediate waveform.
The labels \emph{energy} and \emph{timing} denote the experimental roles of the first-stage
and second-stage waveforms, respectively; they identify successive outputs of this
cascaded readout circuit. The role of electronic readout design in timing has been
investigated in studies of SiPM preamplifiers and TOF-PET signal-processing
chains~\cite{huizenga2012,pourashraf2022}.

The energy output is recorded to retain the energy information required for event
selection. Its complete pulse amplitude lies within the oscilloscope's vertical range,
and the maximum amplitude is used as the energy-selection observable. The intermediate
waveform supplies the energy measurement required in clinical readout. The additional
high-gain postamplification used here is costly and is not part of standard clinical
readout, motivating the application of LED and ML timing methods to the first-stage
waveform as well. Timing analysis of waveforms that also provide energy
information has been investigated in previous work~\cite{berg2018}.

In the present acquisition, the first-stage waveform is cleaner and more stable, but
its leading-edge timing is more affected by time walk. The high-gain second-stage
waveform is more susceptible to noise and waveform instability.
For this output, the oscilloscope vertical range is deliberately much smaller than the
full pulse amplitude. This setting emphasizes the initial rising edge, while the pulse
peak is clipped in amplitude. It provides a timing-oriented acquisition intended to
approach the best timing resolution attainable with the same crystal and SiPM.
A narrow vertical range emphasizing pulse arrival has also been used in the waveform
acquisitions of Onishi et al.~\cite{onishi2022}.

Each dataset consists of coincidence events with four waveforms recorded together:
one intermediate waveform and one final waveform from each detector. Channels 1 and 2
contain the first-stage (energy) waveforms, while channels 3 and 4 contain the corresponding
second-stage (timing) waveforms. The energy and timing modes are therefore derived from the same four-channel
event records. The documented acquisitions use $100\,\mathrm{ns}$ records sampled at $80\,\mathrm{GS\,s}^{-1}$.
Each board is analysed independently, with energy-based event selection common to both
waveform modes. The energy mode examines timing information in the standard first-stage
signal; the timing mode examines the dedicated high-gain, narrow-range acquisition.

\subsection{Acquisitions and scientific roles}

$48\,\mathrm{V}$ for each board. They are assigned to control, development and blind roles,

Control determines the preprocessing and LED selection rules. Development provides all
model training and selection data. The blind acquisition supplies the final test population.
