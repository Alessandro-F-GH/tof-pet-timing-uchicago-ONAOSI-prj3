\section{Results}

The final results are organized around the two-model comparison implemented in the repository: the detector-shared antisymmetric MLP and the paired Onishi CNN reference. For each reported board, waveform family, and bias-voltage set, the two models are run independently with the same fixed LED threshold and the same configured waveform windows. Final performance refers to the permanent blind-test population.

\subsection{Main MLP-versus-Onishi comparison}

The primary result is the blind-test coincidence timing resolution (CTR) of the antisymmetric MLP and the Onishi CNN relative to the common LED reference. The comparison is shown separately for the two predefined waveform windows because changing the temporal context changes the information available to both models.

\subsection{Energy-channel and timing-channel behavior}

The MLP-versus-Onishi comparison is then separated by waveform family. The energy waveform generally starts from a poorer conventional timing baseline and can therefore leave more room for event-by-event correction. The dedicated timing waveform is designed for timing and may show a smaller relative ML gain while still providing the best absolute CTR.

For each waveform family, report both the absolute blind-test CTR of LED, MLP, and Onishi CNN and the improvement of each model relative to the same LED reference. This keeps relative correctability separate from absolute detector timing.

\subsection{LED-threshold sensitivity: MLP-only supporting study}

LED-threshold dependence is no longer part of the main two-model experiment. The repository implements it as a separate \texttt{threshold\_scan} study for the antisymmetric MLP only. For each configured threshold, MLP hyperparameters are selected by validation CTR and the selected checkpoint is evaluated on blind data without refitting.

The scan reports blind LED CTR, blind MLP CTR, and relative improvement for every threshold. It is therefore treated as a sensitivity study and not as a third branch of the model comparison.

\subsection{Model-output correlation}

A similar final CTR does not imply that two models apply the same correction to every event. The \texttt{compare-runs} workflow therefore aligns the persisted blind-test event IDs and computes the Pearson correlation between the MLP and Onishi model outputs at each bias voltage and for each waveform window.

\reportfigure
    {figures/model_output_correlation_onishi_window.pdf}
    {Blind-test correlation between MLP and Onishi model outputs for the Onishi waveform window.}
    {fig:output-correlation-onishi}

\reportfigure
    {figures/model_output_correlation_wide_window.pdf}
    {Blind-test correlation between MLP and Onishi model outputs for the wide waveform window.}
    {fig:output-correlation-wide}

High correlation would indicate that the two architectures recover a similar dominant event-by-event correction despite their different inductive biases. Lower correlation together with similar CTR would instead indicate distinct correction functions with comparable timing performance.

\subsection{Board dependence}

Where both UC and FBK datasets are available, the full MLP-versus-Onishi comparison is repeated independently rather than pooling the electronics. The relevant question is whether the within-board conclusions---relative model performance, dependence on waveform window, and energy-versus-timing behavior---are reproduced across the two front ends.

No cross-board transfer claim is made unless one model is explicitly trained on one board and evaluated on the other.

\subsection{Main result synthesis}

The final synthesis should answer four points without introducing additional model families:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item whether the antisymmetric MLP and paired Onishi CNN differ materially in blind-test CTR;
    \item whether the answer changes between the Onishi and wide waveform windows;
    \item whether the same pattern is observed for energy and timing waveforms and across the available boards;
    \item whether model-output correlation and temporal sensitivity suggest that the two architectures learn the same correction mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

The headline result should therefore be the MLP-versus-Onishi comparison under matched data conditions, with LED retained as the conventional reference rather than as a third machine-learning model.
