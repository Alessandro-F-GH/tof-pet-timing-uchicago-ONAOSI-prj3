\section{Conclusions}
This study demonstrates that waveform-based machine learning can reduce
event-dependent errors in leading-edge timing across two alternative
SiPM readout configurations. The improvements obtained from first-stage
energy waveforms, together with the competitive performance of the
Onishi-inspired CNN on short windows, are qualitatively consistent
with previous waveform-based timing studies~\cite{onishi2022,loignon2025}.
The results show that useful corrections can be learned from digitized
waveforms while retaining the conventional LED measurement and its
fixed-offset calibration.

The detector-shared formulation frequently achieved CTRs close to
those of direct estimators despite enforcing a common detector-level
correction function and detector-exchange antisymmetry. This supports
the feasibility of sharing model parameters between nominally matched
detector channels, potentially reducing the need for separate
pair-specific correction models. Transfer to other physical detector
modules was not evaluated, however, and fixed timing offsets still
require conventional calibration.

Model complexity did not consistently translate into better timing
resolution. Linear ridge regression performed competitively across
the investigated conditions and achieved the lowest test CTR in
several wide-window comparisons; MiniRocket features combined with
ridge regression were also effective. These results indicate that
relatively simple estimators can capture a substantial part of the
correctable waveform dependence. The performance of more complex
networks was configuration-dependent, with occasional weak corrections
or discrepancies between validation and test performance, rather than
a systematic advantage over linear alternatives.

Extending the input from the short leading-edge window to the wide
window improved the best test CTR for both waveform modes on both
boards, although RMSE changes were less uniform. The timing waveforms
were particularly sensitive to the available observation interval:
short crops often offered limited correction, whereas wider crops
allowed stronger improvements. Temporal occlusion showed that energy
models were most sensitive near LED crossing and the early rising edge,
while timing models also responded to later waveform structure.
This late sensitivity was especially evident for the FBK timing
signal around $20\,\mathrm{ns}$. Such maps identify regions affecting
model predictions under the chosen perturbation, not independent or
causally established sources of timing information.

Despite these improvements, the second-stage timing waveforms retained
a marked advantage over the first-stage energy waveforms. The best
wide-window CTRs were $52.9$ and $54.4\,\mathrm{ps}$ for UC and FBK
timing signals, respectively, compared with $105.3$ and
$111.0\,\mathrm{ps}$ for their energy signals. This approximately
twofold difference describes the tested acquisition conditions;
the waveform modes also differ in oscilloscope scaling and selected
event populations. The two boards approached similar corrected
timing CTRs despite different LED baselines, which may be consistent
with residual contributions common to the detector response and
not removed by the investigated waveform corrections. The present
measurements do not isolate such contributions or establish a
universal timing-resolution limit.
