\section{Conclusions}
Waveform-based correction substantially improved leading-edge timing
on the first-stage energy signals of both boards. For the short
Onishi-inspired window, the best test CTR decreased from
$142.2$ to $110.5\,\mathrm{ps}$ on UC ($22.3\%$) and from
$147.3$ to $115.4\,\mathrm{ps}$ on FBK ($21.7\%$).
With the wider window, these reductions reached $25.9\%$ and
$24.6\%$, respectively. The strong performance of the
Onishi-inspired CNN in the short-window energy comparisons is
consistent with previous waveform-based LED correction
studies~\cite{onishi2022,loignon2025}.

The detector-shared formulation retained competitive timing
performance without using separate detector-specific scoring
functions. Across the eight board, mode and window comparisons,
the best shared estimator was within $1.5\,\mathrm{ps}$ of the
overall best in six cases; the largest gap was $3.9\,\mathrm{ps}$
for FBK energy waveforms with the wide window. These are
differences between test CTR point estimates, not paired-bootstrap
significance claims. The results support detector-level parameter
sharing within the tested detector pairs, while neither transfer
to other physical modules nor elimination of conventional
fixed-offset calibration has been demonstrated.

Greater model complexity did not consistently improve CTR. The
lowest test CTR in each of the four wide-window conditions was
obtained either by direct linear ridge regression (UC energy and
FBK timing) or by MiniRocket features followed by ridge regression
(FBK energy and UC timing). For example, UC timing reached
$52.9\,\mathrm{ps}$ with MiniRocket and $53.5\,\mathrm{ps}$
with direct linear ridge. Thus, relatively low-complexity
regression approaches were competitive with the investigated
neural networks, although the best architecture depended on
the experimental condition.

Extending the input window lowered the best test CTR by
$5.2$ and $4.4\,\mathrm{ps}$ in UC and FBK energy mode,
respectively, and by $3.4$ and $3.3\,\mathrm{ps}$ in
timing mode. For UC timing, the best correction relative to LED
increased from $0.8$ to $4.2\,\mathrm{ps}$; for FBK timing,
from $4.3$ to $7.6\,\mathrm{ps}$. Temporal occlusion indicated
that energy-model sensitivity was concentrated near the
LED crossing, whereas the timing models also responded to
later waveform structures. In the additional FBK shared
MiniRocket analysis, the sensitivity peak around
$20$--$21\,\mathrm{ns}$ reached approximately $0.65$ of the
normalized maximum at the crossing. This is evidence of
model sensitivity under occlusion, not a causal attribution
of timing information or proof of an optimal window length.

The choice of readout stage remained consequential after
correction. For the wide window, the best first-stage energy
CTRs were $105.3$ and $111.0\,\mathrm{ps}$, approximately
twice the corresponding second-stage timing CTRs of
$52.9\pm0.8$ and $54.4\pm0.7\,\mathrm{ps}$ on UC and FBK,
respectively. The difference between the two boards' timing
CTRs decreased from $4.9\,\mathrm{ps}$ for LED to
$1.5\,\mathrm{ps}$ after the best corrections. This convergence
may be consistent with residual timing contributions common
to the detector response, but does not establish their
origin or a universal resolution floor. Because the two
waveform modes also have different acquisition scales and
event-selection criteria, the approximately twofold observed
advantage cannot be attributed to amplification alone.
